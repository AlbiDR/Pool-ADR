PLATES AND LABELS

Five photographs, and they carry more weight than their number suggests: every
specification in the survey rests on one of them. If a figure about the filter,
the valve, the pump or the acid is ever in doubt, it is settled here and not by
anything written down.

Ordered along the water path rather than by capture time: filter, then the
valve on top of it, then the pump that feeds it, then the chemical that is
dosed into it.

  01   the Kripsol filter's data plate, filling the frame
  02   the same plate in place on the vessel
  03   the six-way valve dial
  04   the DAB EuroPro 200 M nameplate
  05   the acid drum label

WHY 01 AND 02 ARE BOTH HERE
  01 is readable and 02 is proof. A plate photographed on its own is a
  photograph of a plate; it says what some Kripsol filter is, not what this one
  is. 02 shows the same plate on the red vessel standing in this room, which is
  what ties the 760 column to the filter in the corner. Neither is redundant.

WHAT THE PLATES ACTUALLY SETTLE
  01   The 760 column is the one that applies, and it is the highlighted one on
       the plate: 0.45 m2 of bed, 22 m3/h, 225 kg of 0.4 to 0.8 mm sand. Below
       the model table, applying to the whole range: working pressure 0.5 to
       1.5 kg/cm2, test pressure 3.5, and FILTRATION RATE 50 m3/h per m2.
  03   All seven valve positions, and the sentence cast into the rim in four
       languages: NEVER SHIFT HANDLE WHILE PUMP IS RUNNING. Turning that valve
       against a running pump is what destroys the seal inside it.
  04   EUROPRO 200 M, N. 2.1213, TF 60 S1. Q 31-29-26 m3/h against H 3-10-12 m,
       max 2.5 bar, Hmin 5 m, P2 2 HP, P1 1.9 kW, 220 to 240 V, 8.6 A, and
       2800 rpm and IP55 lower on the plate.
  05   ACIDO SOLFORICO 36%, UN 2796, class 8, H314, 25 kg, Siac srl, sold as
       battery electrolyte.

THE TWO PLATES, READ AGAINST EACH OTHER
  This is what makes 01 and 04 worth more together than apart, and it narrows
  an item the survey lists as open.

  The filter is rated at 22 m3/h, which is 0.45 m2 at the plate's own stated
  50 m3/h per m2. The pump's published curve runs from 31 m3/h at 3 m of head
  down to 26 m3/h at 12 m. So at EVERY point the maker publishes, this pump
  delivers more than the filter is rated for: 26 m3/h through 0.45 m2 is about
  58 m3/h per m2, some 16 percent over the plate rate, and 29 m3/h is 29
  percent over.

  What that does not settle is the duty point, because the system head has
  never been measured and a real installation can sit beyond the published
  curve, where flow falls further. The survey's open list asks for the suction
  pipe's outside diameter for exactly this reason. What the two plates do
  settle is the direction: nothing here suggests this bed is being under-run,
  and the arithmetic says it is probably being over-run. That is consistent
  with the one symptom the survey describes as this pool's weak point, water
  that looks clear but reads faintly cloudy, which is what a sand bed does when
  it is asked to filter faster than it can.

  Read this as an inference from two nameplates, not as a measurement.

THE ONE THAT CONTRADICTS THE PRINTED SHEETS
  05. The sheet in the room calls that container the chlorine drum. The label
  says sulphuric acid. The label wins, and it is not a pedantic win: the
  granular chlorine on the shelf carries EUH031, contact with acids liberates a
  toxic gas, and this room is 4.52 m2 below ground with one door. Filenames and
  house notes are a claim; a label is evidence.

  36% is also above what pool practice recommends. PWTAG puts the ceiling for
  supply to pools at 25%, and 15% w/w is where EU Regulation 2019/1148 begins
  treating sulphuric acid as a restricted explosives precursor. The survey's
  safety notes say what to do about that; this folder only records what it is.
